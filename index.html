Selected work

Nine problems, and what changed

Each started as a vague question and ended in a shipped change. Figures are stated as rates and ratios — the underlying commercial numbers stay with their owners.

The problem. Patient statements were being held whenever the address on file was invalid — but nobody could size how much revenue sat behind that. Without a number, the fix couldn't be prioritised.

What I did. Quantified the population of patients whose statements were blocked, linked those held records to the payments they would plausibly have generated, and worked out which attributes actually predicted recoverability. As the feature moved toward general availability I stabilised the underlying datasets so stakeholders had one reliable version to launch on, then shipped the dashboard tracking monthly recovery.

Large held-statement population ~35% of blocked balances recovered Taken through to GA

The problem. Once a balance reached bad debt, only 2–3% was ever recovered. The working assumption was that patients simply don't pay after write-off.

What I found. Segmenting thousands of practices by collection-policy behaviour told a different story: around 70% of policies were automated, but practices frequently overrode them. Those overrides were pushing genuinely collectable balances into write-off before anyone had a real chance to collect. The problem wasn't patient behaviour — it was configuration drift.

What changed. Practices were alerted to set the minimum-days-before-write-off threshold appropriately, so accounts hand off to collection agencies at the right moment rather than too early.

Thousands of practices analysed ~70% automated policies Recovery 2–3% → 8%

The problem. Money collected from patients was taking weeks to land against the right charge. Everyone knew it was slow; nobody had isolated where the time went.

What I did. Instrumented time-to-post and median days-to-post across the post-collection flow, then analysed the distribution rather than the average — which is where the bottleneck was hiding. That pointed at the posting script as the constraint.

What changed. The analysis informed the dental Patient Wallet build, which now posts 100% of collected payments to the correct charges within 48 hours.

Weeks → under 48 hours 100% posted to correct charge Dental wallet shipped

The problem. Decoupling the payment-card page promised faster checkout, but the team was weighing a single cutover on a path that handles live patient money.

What I did. Measured the decoupling effect on wait time across time-of-service and non-TOS flows, and built a dashboard comparing payment requests received against those successfully posted — so the team could see failure, not just volume.

The recommendation. A phased rollout validated by A/B testing rather than one cutover: lower blast radius on a revenue-critical path, with evidence at each step.

Wait time 8s → 2s 90% daily posting success Phased A/B rollout adopted

The problem. The copay prediction surfaced to users measured around 75% accuracy, and the reported figure wasn't trusted.

What I did. The model wasn't the issue — its inputs were. I isolated adjudicated claims, excluded non-adjudicated instances contaminating the evaluation set, and partnered with the Data Science team to deduplicate prediction records feeding the dashboard.

Result. Measured accuracy rose to 88%, on a number the team could defend. Alongside this I ran a Snowflake cost-reduction workstream — retiring stale Airflow jobs and building a credit-monitoring dashboard for per-job cost visibility.

Accuracy 75% → 88% Adjudicated-claim isolation Prediction-record dedup Snowflake cost monitoring

The problem. The compliance and safety teams were reviewing sellers manually to catch risky behaviour — slow, inconsistent, and impossible to scale with the marketplace.

What I built. A supervised risk-scoring model, owned end to end: engineered features from Hive-sourced seller metadata, then trained and evaluated logistic regression and random forest classifiers against a hypothesis-driven baseline. I layered NLP over seller content — text classification, named entity recognition and sentiment — so behavioural signals in text fed the score alongside structured attributes.

Result. Reviewers were pointed at the sellers that mattered, removing 50% of manual review effort.

50% of manual review removed Logistic regression + random forest NLP: classification, NER, sentiment Hive feature engineering

The problem. Metrics reporting and root-cause analysis were largely manual, and during Big Billion Days the war room needed numbers faster than people could assemble them.

What I did. Automated 60% of manual metrics reporting and RCA into scheduled Power BI dashboards, and built sale-event reporting on GCP with Python so war-room decisions ran on current data rather than yesterday's. I redesigned the fact table schemas behind them for query-efficient storage, and migrated legacy QlikView facts onto internal platforms.

Also. Time-series forecasting (ARIMA, Holt-Winters) of KPI trends for the planning team, plus demand analytics for Flipkart Home Service.

60% of reporting automated 50% less war-room manual effort 50% maintenance cut by migration 10% less compute 20% better manpower utilisation

The work. Built predictive models in Python on pharmaceutical datasets to identify the factors associated with drug success, and developed the preprocessing pipelines that made those datasets usable — cleaning and transforming large, inconsistent clinical and commercial data before analysis.

Analysis. Ran exploratory analysis to surface correlations and outliers, which fed the statistical metrics and KPIs the client relied on. Alongside the modelling I automated department-wide allocation and productivity tracking in a VBA application with Power BI reporting.

Predictive factors identified Preprocessing pipelines EDA: correlations & outliers KPI automation

The context. Trial master file data in clinical research has to be right — regulators audit it. Three and a half years here is where I learned that a number is only as good as the rules that produced it.

The work. Implemented data quality rules and standards inside the data model to enforce accuracy, consistency and reliability, and worked with business analysts and the operations team to translate requirements into data structures rather than one-off extracts. Ran data collection, preparation and wrangling, plus team-level A/B testing and statistical reasoning to support application development.

Why it still matters. The instinct to test assumptions before reporting a number — the thing stakeholders later valued most — started here.
